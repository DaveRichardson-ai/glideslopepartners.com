## A number that sat beside the proceeds

When Suncorp announced the sale of its bank to ANZ in July 2022, the headline price was A$4.9 billion and estimated net proceeds were A$4.1 billion. The same presentation carried a third number: stranded costs of about A$40 million a year after tax, net of TSA income, which Suncorp aimed to remove within three years of completion.

That disclosure is unusual for being made on signing day, which means the cost had been sized before terms were agreed. In most carve-outs the seller's residual cost base is left to the finance team after completion. By then the price is fixed, the TSA fees are agreed and the separation plan is set.

The Suncorp figure also shows the scale. If none of that cost came out until the third year, the cumulative drag would be about A$120 million after tax, on top of the A$0.8 billion already taken off the headline price. That is our arithmetic on Suncorp's published figures.

## What it is, and two dates worth focusing on

Stranded cost is the part of the seller's cost base that supported the divested business and does not leave with it. It arrives on two dates. Some strands at completion, and the rest is hidden by TSA income and strands when the TSA ends, which is why Suncorp quoted its figure net of TSA.

A TSA priced at cost-plus recovers cost while it runs. When it ends the cost is still there, unless the seller has a plan to remove it on the same timetable.

## The same scale is paid for twice

The buyer has the mirror problem. A business that leaves a group loses the parent's scale and has to replace it: its own licences, its own control functions, its own vendor pricing. That is standalone cost.

The seller's stranded cost and the buyer's standalone cost do not net to zero. The seller keeps fixed cost it can no longer spread, and the buyer builds or buys capacity it previously shared. Added together they exceed what the business cost inside the group. A carve-out creates that gap, the TSA defers it, and price and terms decide who carries it.

## Where it comes from in financial services

Financial services groups run on shared platforms and shared control functions, and regulation makes some of that cost harder to remove.

| Source | Why it strands |
| --- | --- |
| Technology licences and infrastructure | Enterprise licences and data centre capacity are priced on group volumes and cannot be cut in proportion |
| Control functions | Risk, compliance, audit, finance and legal are sized for the group; regulatory obligations do not shrink with revenue |
| Property | Floors and sites were shared; leases run on |
| Shared operations | Contact centres, payments and back-office teams lose volume but keep fixed overhead |
| Vendor contracts | Minimum commitments and volume tiers were negotiated for the whole group |
| TSA delivery | Staff and systems kept to deliver TSAs can only be removed when the TSAs exit. Where the buyer is APRA-regulated, the seller is likely to be a material service provider under CPS 230 and has to deliver to that standard |

The CPS 230 point is our reading of the standard. It adds cost to TSA delivery that a cost-plus fee based on historical allocations will not capture.

## What the public record shows

McKinsey's study of 144 large spin-offs found that parent companies can take one to three years to recover after a divestiture. It also found that cost allocations are an unreliable guide: in one case detailed analysis put the divested unit's overheads tens of millions of dollars below what the allocations showed.

AMP's results show how stranded cost accumulates across successive transactions. Its FY23 result recorded A$20 million of stranded costs from the AMP Capital sales. A year later, after the advice sale, management told analysts that A$45 million of stranded costs made up a large part of the cost reduction planned for FY25, and that those costs were being held at the corporate centre.

Three years is the common figure across these sources. It is longer than most TSAs and longer than most deal models carry the cost.

## Why it belongs in price and deal terms

For a seller, stranded cost is part of the real proceeds of a sale, and boards should see it beside the headline price when they decide. It also bears on four terms that are usually settled quickly.

- **TSA pricing.** If cost-plus does not cover what the seller must carry to deliver the services, the seller subsidises the buyer for the life of the TSA.
- **TSA duration and extensions.** Every extension delays the seller's cost-out. Extension pricing should step up to reflect that and incentivise action to separate on the buyer side.
- **Exit obligations.** Firm exit dates, with the buyer carrying the cost of late exit, protect the seller's cost-out plan.
- **Reverse TSAs.** Services the seller still needs from the divested business create cost the seller does not control.

## How to size it in two weeks

A usable estimate does not need a full cost allocation study. It needs finance, technology and procurement in the room, and five steps.

1. **Start from the allocation, then test it.** Take the cost allocated to the divested business in management accounts and split it into direct cost, which leaves, and allocated cost, which stays. Check the largest allocated lines against their real drivers, because allocations can be wrong in either direction.
2. **Test each line for variability.** For each, ask what share can be removed within twelve months of TSA exit and what it takes: contract renegotiation, headcount reduction, site consolidation or platform decommissioning.
3. **Map against the TSA schedule.** Cost that supports TSA services cannot come out until those services exit. Put the cost-out timeline on the same page as the TSA exit plan.
4. **Separate one-off from run-rate.** Decommissioning, redundancy and contract exit costs are one-off. What remains after them is the stranded run-rate.
5. **Put a range on it.** A low and a high estimate, with the main assumptions stated, is enough to inform price and terms.

The output fits on one page for the board: stranded run-rate at completion and at TSA exit, the one-off cost to remove it, the timetable, and the deal terms that protect it.

## The buyer's side

Information memoranda tend to present a carve-out on its historical allocated cost. That number does not often hold once the business has standalone vendor pricing, its own control functions and a new technology estate. Private equity buyers, with no parent to absorb the business, should treat standalone cost as a core valuation input and test it against the TSA scope.

On both sides the work is the same: establish what the business depends on, and what that costs once the dependency ends. Done before signing, the answer can still move the price.
