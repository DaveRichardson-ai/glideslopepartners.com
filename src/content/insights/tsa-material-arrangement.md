## The grace period is over

Since 1 July 2026, every arrangement an APRA-regulated entity has with a material service provider must meet CPS 230. Pre-existing contracts had relief until the earlier of their next renewal or that date. That relief has now ended for all of them.

Transitional service agreements were rarely drafted with CPS 230 in mind. Many in force today were signed before the standard commenced, or were drafted quickly at signing as schedules to the sale agreement. If a TSA supports a critical operation, it is almost certainly a material arrangement, and it now has to look like one.

## Why a TSA counts

CPS 230 does not carve out arrangements between related or formerly related parties. What matters is whether the regulated entity relies on the service to run a critical operation, or to manage a material operational risk. A TSA under which a former parent runs the buyer's core banking, policy administration, member registry, payments or data centre meets that test on day one.

The consequences follow directly:

- The TSA provider is a material service provider and belongs on the entity's register.
- The agreement needs the contractual content CPS 230 requires of any material arrangement.
- APRA must be notified within 20 business days of entering into or materially changing an agreement that supports a critical operation. Signing a TSA, extending it, or changing its service scope can each trigger that notice.
- Offshoring through a TSA (for example, where the former parent runs the service from an offshore hub) carries a prior-notification expectation [verify current wording].
- The TSA must sit inside the entity's business continuity planning, with tolerance levels for the critical operations it supports.

Both sides are affected. The buyer relies on the services. The seller, if it is itself APRA-regulated, may depend on reverse TSAs for services the divested business keeps providing back.

## Where typical TSAs fall short

TSAs are written to keep a business running for twelve to twenty-four months while the buyer builds its own capability. They tend to be short, priced at cost-plus and silent on anything that looks like ongoing vendor management.

| CPS 230 expectation | Common TSA position |
| --- | --- |
| Defined services and service levels | Services described as "as provided in the 12 months before completion", with no measurable service levels |
| Performance reporting | Monthly governance meeting, no reporting obligation |
| Data ownership, location and return | Data handled under the sale agreement, not the TSA; return mechanics unclear |
| Audit and access rights | Limited or absent; APRA access rights rarely spelled out |
| Subcontracting notice | Silent, because the provider uses its own vendors and assumes it can keep doing so |
| Business continuity | "Same standard as for the provider's own business", with no tolerances agreed |
| Termination and transition-out | Exit dates fixed, but transition-out assistance thin and priced separately |

The gap that most often causes trouble is the third one. When a TSA ends, the buyer needs its data back in a usable form, and the seller needs to delete what it no longer has a right to hold. If the TSA does not say how that happens, it is negotiated under time pressure at the end.

## What to fix

**If you are relying on a TSA today (buyer)**

- Map each TSA service to the critical operations it supports and record the provider on the material service provider register
- Confirm APRA was notified when the TSA was signed, extended or re-scoped, and notify now if it was not
- Agree measurable service levels and a reporting pack for the services that support critical operations
- Document data location, access and the return-and-destroy process at exit
- Bring the TSA into business continuity testing, with tolerance levels agreed with the provider
- Re-baseline the exit plan, including transition-out assistance and its cost

**If you are providing TSA services (seller)**

- Check whether any reverse TSAs make the buyer a material service provider to you
- List the subcontractors used to deliver TSA services, since your vendors are now the buyer's fourth parties
- Price the extra reporting, audit support and continuity testing the buyer will ask for, before they ask

**At signing (both)**

- Draft TSA schedules to CPS 230 from the start, rather than amending after completion
- Agree who notifies APRA, and when, as part of the completion checklist

## Implications for deals in progress

For transactions now in negotiation, CPS 230 changes the economics of TSAs. Compliant TSAs cost more to provide and take longer to agree, which affects both the TSA fee and the timetable to completion. Sellers that treat TSAs as a cost-recovery afterthought will find buyers pricing the compliance gap into the deal.

The practical response is to bring TSA design forward into diligence and treat it as a workstream with its own owner, not a schedule the lawyers finish the week before signing. APRA has also consulted on targeted amendments to CPS 230 covering certain non-traditional service providers such as exchanges and payment schemes [check final position]. Those changes do not touch TSAs between commercial parties.
