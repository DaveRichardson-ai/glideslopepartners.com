## Wealth is back on the bank agenda

ANZ's FY26 result on 9 November put wealth back on the table for Australian banks. Cash profit rose 31% to A$7.6 billion, and coverage of the result described the next phase of the 2030 strategy as a growth bet, with wealth and the mass-affluent segment at its centre.

That is a reversal. Between 2018 and 2023 every major bank sold or wound down most of its wealth business. ANZ was early and thorough: it sold its pensions and investments business to IOOF (now Insignia), its life insurer to Zurich, its advice business to Zurich and its margin lending book to Bendigo and Adelaide Bank, and then told the market it had removed wealth management risk for shareholders.

The question for any bank now considering re-entry is less whether wealth is attractive and more what it costs to put back together what the exits took apart. Our partners ran several of those separations from inside the institutions being separated. This paper sets out what we learned about the entanglements, and what they imply for a bank choosing between building, partnering and buying.

## What the exits actually separated

The exits did not remove one business. They cut eight kinds of connection between a bank and its wealth operations, and each one has to be rebuilt, rented or deliberately left out on the way back.

| Connection | What the exits separated | What re-entry has to decide |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Licences | AFSLs, RSE licences and trustee boards moved to the buyer | Whether the bank holds licences again, or distributes under someone else's |
| Trustee governance | Related-party arrangements between bank and trustee were unwound | How to meet trustee conflict obligations if the bank is both distributor and service provider |
| Platforms and administration | Wrap and master trust platforms, registries and admin contracts moved, usually under long TSAs | Own, rent or partner for the platform; who carries the technology debt |
| Advice and distribution | Employed planners, referral flows from branches and relationship managers, and dealer group links were cut | Employed advisers, a referral partnership, or digital advice only |
| Customer data and consent | Customer records were split, with consent and privacy positions set per business | What data can flow between bank and wealth provider, and on what consent basis |
| Remediation | Fee-for-no-service and other remediation stayed with the vendor, typically backed by indemnities | Whether an acquisition re-imports historical conduct exposure |
| Shared services | Finance, risk, HR, technology infrastructure and procurement were stood up separately or provided under TSA | Which services the bank provides, and whether those are now material arrangements under CPS 230 |
| Brand | Bank brands were removed from products, statements and marketing | Co-branding, white-labelling or full bank branding, and the conduct risk each carries |

The remediation and trustee rows are where the exits took longest, and they are the rows a re-entry business case most often understates.

## Three routes back in

A bank can build, partner or buy. Each route rebuilds a different subset of the connections above, and each moves the hard work to a different point in time.

| | Build | Partner | Buy |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| What it looks like | Private bank and relationship-manager growth, in-house investment products, digital advice | Referral or white-label agreement with a platform, super fund or advice network | Acquire a platform, adviser network, super business or a whole wealth group |
| Connections rebuilt | Distribution, brand, some data | Distribution and data, under contract | Most or all eight |
| Where the work sits | Operating model and hiring, over years | Contract design, data flows and conduct controls, up front | Diligence, separation from the vendor, then integration |
| Regulatory weight | Lowest; within existing licences | Moderate; CPS 230 material arrangement if the bank relies on the partner | Highest; licences, trustee obligations, APRA and ASIC approvals, historical conduct |
| Speed to scale | Slowest | Fast to launch, capped by the partner | Fastest to scale, slowest to stabilise |

ANZ's stated strategy so far points to the build route: more relationship managers and differentiated propositions for mass-affluent customers. The buy route is where the market speculation sits. Our view is that the partner route is under-discussed. It gives a bank distribution economics without re-importing trustee and remediation exposure, but only if the agreement is designed as carefully as a TSA, because in practice that is what it becomes.

An acquisition of a listed wealth group is unlikely to be a clean single transaction. More probably it would be a break-up in which different buyers take the bank, platform, super and advice components. Every component then needs to be separated from the others before it can be integrated anywhere.

## What the business case usually misses

Re-entry business cases are built around revenue, deposits and cross-sell. The costs that decide whether they hold come from the separation history of whatever the bank is buying or partnering with.

1. **The vendor's TSAs.** A wealth business bought out of a larger group is often still running on its former parent's services. Those TSAs have exit dates, step-up pricing and services nobody fully documented. The buyer inherits the exit programme.
2. **Stranded and standalone cost.** Platforms and super businesses carved out in the last five years were sized for a parent that no longer exists. Their standalone cost base is rarely what the information memorandum shows.
3. **Trustee independence.** If the bank both distributes a super product and provides services to its trustee, the trustee must manage that conflict. That shapes the deal structure and the service agreements, not only the governance chart.
4. **Remediation tails.** Indemnities from earlier sales may cap, expire or sit with an entity that no longer has the balance sheet. Diligence has to trace each one.
5. **Data and consent.** The commercial logic of re-entry depends on using bank data to serve wealth customers. Whether consent and privacy positions allow that is a legal question to answer before the price is set.
6. **Integration capacity.** A bank already integrating a major acquisition is drawing on the same programme leaders, technology teams and risk functions a wealth deal would need. ANZ, for example, is still integrating Suncorp Bank, with full integration targeted for mid-2027 [verify against latest ANZ disclosure].

## Questions for the board before choosing a route

1. Which of the eight connections do we need to own to make the economics work, and which can we rent?
2. Are we prepared to hold trustee and licence obligations again, and what conduct exposure would come with them?
3. If we buy, what is the target still taking from its former parent, and what does exiting those services cost?
4. If we partner, is the partner a material service provider under CPS 230, and does the agreement give us the exit rights that implies?
5. Do we have the programme capacity to run this alongside our current integrations, or does it wait?

Answering these properly takes transition specialists in the room during diligence, not after signing. That is the point at which the answers still change the price, the structure or the decision.
