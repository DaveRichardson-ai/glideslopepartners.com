ANZ chief executive Nuno Matos told the Australian Financial Review on 9 November that wealth management has to return to banking, and that regulation has to change for advice to be delivered properly. Some of it already has. More is promised. The most recent announcement, though, leaves banks out.

A bank weighing re-entry needs to separate three layers: what has been added since the exits of 2018 to 2023, what has been relaxed, and what has been announced without legislation.

## Added since the exits

Grandfathered commissions ended in 2021. The design and distribution obligations, a tighter hawking prohibition and a broader breach reporting regime all started the same year. The Financial Accountability Regime has applied to banks since 2024 and to super trustees and insurers since 2025, which makes named executives accountable for conduct in each business. CPS 230 took effect in July 2025 and governs any material service arrangement between a bank and a wealth provider.

The Compensation Scheme of Last Resort has operated since 2024 and is funded by industry levies. The special levy attributed to financial advice for 2026–27 is A$170.3 million.

## Relaxed

The first tranche of the Delivering Better Financial Outcomes reforms became law in July 2024. It removed fee disclosure statements, simplified ongoing fee consent, allowed financial services guides to be published online and gave legal certainty to paying advice fees from super. These changes lower the cost of giving advice. Who is permitted to give it stayed the same.

## Announced, not yet legislated

On 19 August 2026 the Assistant Treasurer, Daniel Mulino, set out the rest at the National Press Club. A new class of adviser for simpler advice will be "initially limited to APRA-regulated superannuation funds and life insurers", with commissions, bonuses and volume-based payments prohibited and the scope reviewed after three years. Banks and advice licensees are outside the initial scope.

The government also plans to amend the best interests duty so scaled advice is easier to give, streamline statements of advice, and let super funds prompt members at decision points. No bill has been released and the minister has given no firm timetable.

The same speech responded to the Shield and First Guardian collapses. Platform trustees would have to compensate members' full capital losses where the trustee has breached its obligations, and show they can fund that from their own capital or a related-party guarantee. Treasury consulted in April 2026 on codified due diligence for platform investment menus and per-member holding limits. The hawking exemption for advice is to narrow to existing client relationships. The detail of all three is still being designed.

## What this means for a bank

Three implications follow. They are our reading of announced policy, and the legislation may differ.

- The simpler-advice channel now runs through an RSE licence or a life company. Those are the licences the banks sold. A bank that wants that channel has to acquire or partner with a fund or insurer, or wait for the three-year review.
- Owning a platform is becoming a balance-sheet commitment. If a platform trustee must show it can compensate members, and a related-party guarantee is one way to do that, a bank owner should expect to be asked for one. That belongs in the price and in the capital plan.
- The timing is awkward. ANZ has said its growth phase starts in FY28. A bank on that timetable would choose its route during 2027, probably before the advice legislation passes.

Referral models raise a further question for counsel: whether a bank customer referred to a partner's adviser falls inside the narrower hawking exemption, given the customer is not yet that adviser's client.

## What to do with this

Choose a route that works under current law and can adjust. In practice that favours partnering with a super fund or insurer over buying a platform, at least until the compensation design is known. It also means writing change-in-law and exit provisions into any partner agreement now.

Three things are worth watching: the exposure draft of the advice bill, the final shape of the platform trustee compensation obligation, and whether the three-year review of the adviser class is given a fixed date.
